Keeping a garden notebook

Every spring I promise myself that this year I will remember which tomatoes split in the August rain, and every spring I have forgotten by the time the seed catalogues arrive. The notebook started as a way to stop repeating the same mistakes. It turned into the most useful tool in the shed, more useful than the good secateurs and far cheaper than the greenhouse I keep pricing and never buy.

The rules are simple. One page per bed, one line per visit, and a date on every line. I write what I sowed, what came up, what the slugs ate and what the weather did that week. I do not write how I felt about any of it, because in March the feelings are useless and the dates are everything. A frost on the twelfth of May tells me more about next year than a paragraph of disappointment.

After three seasons the pages start to argue with each other. The beans always sulk in the bed by the fence, which turns out to be the bed that stays wet longest after rain. The courgettes that I swore were a new variety were the same ones from the year before, bought from a different shop with a different label. Patterns that I would have sworn did not exist sit there in pencil, three years in a row.

The notebook also makes the garden less precious. When a crop fails I write it down and move on, because the failure is now information instead of a verdict. Some of the best lines in it are the ones that read only "nothing, again". They saved me a fourth year of the same seed in the same corner, and they made room for something that actually liked the shade.

If you start one, start small. A cheap notebook that fits in a coat pocket will be used; a beautiful one that lives on a shelf will not. Write in pencil, because ink runs when the page gets wet, and it will. Keep it by the back door. The habit matters more than the method, and the method will find you after a season or two of honest, boring entries.
