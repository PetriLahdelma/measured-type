Notes from the morning ferry

A year of crossings between the mainland and the outer islands, written down one journey at a time.

The first ferry leaves the harbour at a quarter past six, and in winter it leaves in the dark. The deck lights come on before the engine does, so for a few minutes the boat glows at the quay like a lit window in an empty street. Most of the passengers are the same each week: two nurses going out to the care home on the far island, a man who repairs outboard motors and carries his tools in a canvas bag, and a schoolteacher who marks homework on the bench by the stairs.

Nobody talks much on the way out. The crossing takes forty minutes when the sea is calm and nearly an hour when the wind comes from the west, and people seem to treat it as time that belongs to no one. They read, they sleep with their heads against the glass, they watch the channel markers go past one by one. The captain once told me that he can tell the weather for the whole day from how many people stand outside on the first crossing.

In spring the route changes its character completely. The ice goes out of the bays in a single week, and suddenly there are birds everywhere: eiders in long dark lines low over the water, terns that hang in the air above the wake, a white-tailed eagle that sits on the same rock every morning as if it had an appointment. The islanders start carrying seedlings and paint tins instead of groceries, and the car deck fills with trailers loaded with timber for summer cottages.

Summer belongs to the visitors. The timetable doubles, the café opens on the upper deck and the queue for the second ferry reaches back past the fish market. Children lean over the rail to look for jellyfish, and their parents photograph the lighthouse from exactly the same place every year. It would be easy to resent the crowds, but the islands depend on them, and the shop on the far quay takes more money in July than in the rest of the year together.

Autumn is the season I like best. The visitors leave in the last week of August as if a bell had rung, the timetable shrinks back to four crossings a day and the light becomes low and yellow. The sea is often rough, and the boat rolls enough that people hold their coffee with both hands. On the quiet crossings the crew let me stand at the back of the bridge, where the radar draws the islands as green shapes that slowly turn as the boat changes course.

By November the first crossing is dark again, and the circle closes. I have made the journey more than three hundred times now, and I still do not know the names of half the islands we pass. What I do know is the sound the ramp makes when it lands on the far quay, the smell of diesel and wet rope, and the particular quiet of a boat full of people who have decided, for forty minutes, that there is nothing they need to do.
